Chapter 2 — The Choice

Three seconds.

Jack stared at the blue screen hovering in front of him, his mind trying to do ten things at once. The numbers were counting down in the corner of the blue screen, and they were not waiting for him to catch up. Three. Two. The bullet that should have been in his brain already was suspended an inch away from his face, just floating there, like someone had paused the worst moment of his life.

This is not real. This is not real. My brain is dying and it is making me see things. That is all this is.

He had read about it somewhere, maybe in one of the neuroscience papers for his own research, how the brain floods with chemicals at the moment of death, how people report seeing tunnels and lights and dead relatives. Maybe this was that. A very British, very specifically Jack version of a near-death hallucination, complete with its own heads up display and legal wording. His brain, even in death, could not stop trying to be clever.

But the dust. That was the thing that did not fit. The dust in the air was not moving. Jack could see individual particles suspended above a toppled stack of books, and they were just there, not falling, not drifting. His brain was not creating that level of detail. His brain could not be arsed with that level of detail.

One second.

"Option two," Jack said, the word barely making it out of his mouth. The taste of blood was on his tongue. "Two. I choose two."

He was not sure how he had spoken at all, because he did not feel like he had a body anymore, not really. He felt the way a memory feels when you are trying to hold on to it before sleep.

The screen flickered. The timer stopped. And then a new block of text unfolded itself on top of the last one, neat and businesslike, as if the System had just pulled up the next form in the pile.

SYSTEM NOTIFICATION

Anti-Hiero Protocol — Registration

Welcome, candidate.

To complete activation, you must accept the Terms of Service and User Agreement.

[View full Terms of Service]
[Accept All]
[Decline — default to death]
Time remaining: 00:00:05

Jack wanted to laugh, or he wanted to cry, and somewhere in between he just wanted it to be over. Terms of Service. He was dying on the floor of his university library with a bullet hovering beside his head, and the thing that had decided to save him was asking him to click Accept All. It was so absurd, so deeply, stupidly modern, that for a split second he almost chose Decline just to spite it.

But he did not. Of course he did not.

The phantom pain was creeping back. His shoulder and his side were waking up to themselves, remembering they were supposed to hurt. His vision was tunnelling at the edges. He did not have five seconds, he had maybe two, and there was no way in the world he was reading a legal document right now. Nobody reads those anyway. Nobody had ever read those. He clicked Accept All on every cookie banner, every app, every update, every form. Everyone did. It was the oldest lie on the internet.

"Accept All." He said it out loud.

The screen flashed once, very hard, and the world went white.

SYSTEM NOTIFICATION

Terms Accepted — Protocol Active

You are now bound by the Anti-Hiero Covenant.

• Initialising temporal reversion…
• Restoring physical integrity…
Birth sign: Taurus (May 15)
Temporal alignment: Perfect (20 years, to the minute)
Assigning designation…
Designation: TAURUS
Status: Active Anti-Hiero

This designation is your signature and your identifier within the Anti-Hiero network.

Welcome, Anti-Hiero Taurus.

Taurus. It was the sort of word that would have embarrassed him on any normal day of his life, the sort of word that belonged in a terrible horoscope column in a magazine Claire would flick through while waiting for her nails to dry. Taurus, the bull. Stubborn. Dependable. Practical. His mother had mentioned it once or twice over the years, usually when she was accusing him of being difficult. And now, apparently, a thing inside the walls of reality had decided it was his name.

Because I was born on the fifteenth of May.

Because I was going to die on the fifteenth of May.

Because the times matched exactly, to the minute, twenty years apart.

The thought sent a cold line down his spine, colder than the blood had been, because it was not random. None of this was random. Whatever the Anti-Hiero System was, it had been watching for that alignment, and it had found him. Which meant there were probably others. It had to have chosen others before him. People who had blinked in libraries and kitchens and hospital beds and said yes to a blue box.

The white light folded in on itself, and Jack blinked.

He was sitting at his desk.

His laptop was open, his highlighter in his hand, his notes spread in the exact positions he remembered from before. His chair was upright. His spine was upright. His hands were his own. The second floor of the Cambridge University Library on West Road was quiet in the tense, pre-exam way that was normal for the time of year, and the clock on the wall read 2:47 PM.

At 3:15 PM, the shooter would enter.

Jack did not move for a long moment. He was afraid that if he moved too fast, the moment would pop like a bubble and he would be back on the floor with the bullet, and he would not get another choice. He breathed. He felt the breath go in and come out. He put a hand on his chest, slowly, carefully, as if he was checking a sleeping animal. No blood. No hole. No pain.

I am back. It actually worked.

The relief came out of him before he could stop it. A laugh, a proper one, loud and startled and completely out of place, the kind of sound that did not belong in a Cambridge library on a Thursday in May. Heads turned. Three of them, at least. The girl at the next desk gave him a very specific British look over the top of her glasses, the one that meant seriously?. From somewhere further back, someone made a sharp "pst!" through their teeth, the universal reading-room shut-up.

"Sorry," Jack mouthed, holding a hand up in apology. "Sorry, sorry."

A librarian at the far end lifted her head, considered him for a second, and went back to whatever she was doing. Heads dropped. He looked at his own hands on the desk. They were shaking a little, but they were there.

The library looked obscenely normal. Sarah Chen, two tables away, was scowling at her calculus notes and chewing on the end of a biro. Someone at the computer station was typing so hard it was borderline percussive. Near the window, a pair of second-year girls were giggling about something on a phone. No one knew. No one had any idea that in twenty-eight minutes, half of them would be on the floor, and the other half would be watching their friends bleed out. It was offensive, almost, how normal they looked. How normal he was supposed to look, on top of everything.

What did I just agree to?

He did not even know what he had accepted. He had pressed a button on a hallucination, and a hallucination had brought him back in time twenty-eight minutes, and now he was sitting in a library with a new name and no idea what came next. His research papers were still open in front of him. The paragraph on neural integration was still half-highlighted. On any other day, that would have been his entire world for the afternoon. Today it was irrelevant. Today he was, apparently, Taurus.

Or was he?

The thought landed with a kind of suspicious, hopeful weight. Maybe I imagined all of it. Maybe he had dozed off over his notes for a second, the way you sometimes did in the library when the radiator was on too high and the air was too warm, and his stressed, overworked brain had served him a very detailed, very stupid nightmare about being shot on his birthday. Maybe in a minute he would look up again and the clock would still say 2:47 because it had never not said 2:47. Maybe Claire had never come over at all. Maybe nobody had died. Maybe the only person who had ever been going to die on his twentieth birthday was a version of Jack that had only ever existed inside a three-second panic attack.

He let himself believe for about two seconds, because believing it was the nicest thing anyone had done for him today.

Then a blue glow bloomed across his notes.

That specific, translucent, impossible blue. Right there on top of his highlighted paragraph about consciousness mapping, glowing like nothing in a normal library had any right to glow.

Jack's stomach went out from under him.

"Crap," he whispered, under his breath, to nobody. "It's real."

He looked up.

QUEST ASSIGNED

Prevent the Massacre

Type: Main Quest (Time-Limited)
Difficulty: Extreme
Objective: Find and stop the shooter before any innocent is harmed.
Time remaining: 00:28:13
Failure conditions:
• Any innocent death
• Expiry of the timer
Penalty for failure: Immediate termination. Contract voided. You will be erased from the record.
Success reward: Survival. Progression. Further quests unlocked.

Jack read it twice. The first time he read it as if it were a note a friend had left him. The second time he read it as a threat.

Erased from record. He was not sure what that meant and he was not in a hurry to find out. It had the flavour of something very old and very uninterested in him as a person. The kind of clause buried at the bottom of page fifty-seven of a contract he had not read, which, to be fair to the universe, he had chosen not to read.

Twenty-eight minutes. He had twenty-eight minutes to find a person he had not even seen yet, not really, not in a way that would hold up if someone asked him to describe them. He had seen a muzzle flash and a gloved hand. He had seen the face for a split second, at the end, smiling. You tried to be a hero, that was so stupid. The voice came back to him as if it had never left, because in some sense it had not. He had heard it about three minutes ago, in a different version of reality.

Do I just shout it? Do I just stand on a chair and shout, "There is going to be a shooting in twenty-eight minutes, please leave"?

He pictured it for about half a second. He pictured the faces. Sarah Chen would look up, irritated. Someone would laugh. Someone else would go and tell a librarian, and the librarian would ring security, and security would very politely ask Jack to come with them, and then, because security was not on the ground floor, the shooter would stroll out of the library, exactly on schedule and do what they had always been going to do. Except this time Jack would be in a small beige office explaining himself to a nice woman with a lanyard, and there would be no second rewind.

No. I cannot warn them. I have to go first.

A small icon folded itself into the bottom corner of his vision, and a wireframe of the library unfolded around it. Three floors, stacked. The second floor, where he was, was populated with a scatter of pale dots, each one a student, each one a person who was sipping coffee or resenting their coursework and would not even remember this afternoon if he did his job correctly. On the basement level, one dot was red.

The red dot was not moving.

They are already here. They are waiting.

Jack closed his laptop slowly, methodically, the way he did every other afternoon. He tucked his pens into his pencil case. He zipped his backpack. He did all of it at normal speed, because the last thing he needed was to look like the strange man from the second floor who had suddenly gone sprinting down the stairs. He lifted his bag onto his shoulder, nodded to no one in particular, and walked.

The stairwell was concrete and cold, and his footsteps sounded loud to him even though they would have been perfectly ordinary to anyone else. The fluorescent lights buzzed, that specific public-building buzz. On the minimap, the red dot stayed still. Waiting. Checking something, maybe. A phone. A list.

Jack reached the basement level and paused at the door. He could hear the ventilation system breathing. He could hear his own heartbeat in his ears, very clearly, as if it was being amplified for the benefit of anyone who might be listening. The corridor beyond the door was empty. Storage rooms on one side. A row of small study carrels on the other. At the far end, a closed door, and behind that door, according to his brand new hallucination minimap, the person who had killed him.

He walked. He tried very hard to walk like a person who was looking for a book.

Through the narrow glass panel in the study-room door, he could see them.

They were sitting. That was the first thing that did not match his memory. In his memory the shooter had been on their feet, moving, hunting. This version of them was just sitting, hunched over a phone, their knee jittering against the table leg. A black hoodie, the hood up. A backpack on the floor beside them, much too heavy, the shape of it wrong for books. From this angle Jack could see only the side of their face. They looked younger than he had expected. Scared, even. A kid. A kid who had not done it yet, who had not yet decided to go up the stairs.

I could leave.

The thought arrived before he could stop it. He could turn around. He could go back up. He could pull the fire alarm on the main floor, empty the library, and let security find this person sitting in a study room with a rucksack full of God-knew-what. The shooter would be caught. No one would die. Jack could go home and pretend none of this had happened, and at 3:15 PM, nothing would happen at all.

Except that a quest notification had told him what would happen if he failed, and it had used the word terminated, and he had a reasonably strong suspicion that the System would not accept "I pulled a fire alarm" as a successful outcome. The System had shown him two options. Kill, or attempt non-lethal subdue with an eighty-seven percent failure rate. It had not offered him a third box labelled to involve the proper authorities.

It wants me to do it myself. That is the test.

He felt sick. He had never even been in a real fight. He had played football in secondary school and lost his temper once at a lad called Miles in year ten and that was the full history of Jack Thomas, violence. And now a thing that lived in the walls of reality wanted him to go into that room and end a person.

His hand was on the door handle. He did not remember deciding to put it there. The handle was cold.

He pushed.

The figure looked up fast, too fast, and Jack saw the full face for the first time. Young. Maybe his own age. Red-rimmed eyes. Pale, patchy skin. A face that had not slept in days.

"Who the fuck are you," they said, not as a question.

Jack's mouth was completely dry. His brain, which had been running on adrenaline and panic for the last five minutes, suddenly could not think of a single useful word. He did the thing Claire would have done, which surprised him, because Claire had been, technically, dead ten minutes ago.

"Sorry, mate," Jack said, forcing a stupid grin onto his face. "Looking for the loo. Is it down here?"

He took a step into the room as he said it, closing the distance, trying to look harmless, lost, a bit dim. The figure's hand was already sliding into the side pocket of the backpack. Jack could see the outline of what was in there. He had seen it recently. Very recently.

"Upstairs," the figure said. Their voice was tight. Their hand had stopped moving in the backpack. It was holding something. "Get out."

"Right, yeah, sorry," Jack said, and he took another step forward.

The figure stood up. The hand came out of the backpack with a pistol in it, the muzzle rising, and Jack did not think, Jack just moved, the way he had moved in the last version of this afternoon, except this time his body already knew it had been shot before, and it seemed angrier about it. He lunged across the table. He got both hands onto the gun and twisted it up and sideways, and the figure shoved back at him with a shocking strength, shouting something that was not really words.

The gun went off. The sound in the small room was immense, a physical thing that slapped the air. The bullet went into the wall behind Jack's shoulder and threw plaster across the table. His ears rang. His vision blurred. The figure was still holding the gun. Jack was still holding their wrist. They were tangled together over the table like wrestlers.

I am going to lose this. I cannot hold on to this.

A new block of blue bloomed in the corner of his eye, impossible, brighter than the strip lights.
